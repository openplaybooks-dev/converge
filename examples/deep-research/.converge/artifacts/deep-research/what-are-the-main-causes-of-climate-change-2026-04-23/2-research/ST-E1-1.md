# Research: Main Causes of Climate Change

**Subtopic ID**: ST-E1-1
**Epoch**: 1
**Research Date**: 2026-04-23

## Overview

Climate change is primarily driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, with carbon dioxide (CO2) from fossil fuel combustion being the dominant contributor. Since the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric CO2 concentrations have increased from approximately 280 parts per million (ppm) to over 420 ppm as of 2023, representing a 50% increase. This unprecedented rise correlates directly with human activities including energy production, transportation, industrial processes, and land-use changes. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has established with very high confidence that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, with observed warming of approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels. Beyond CO2, other greenhouse gases including methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and fluorinated gases contribute significantly to radiative forcing. Methane, though shorter-lived than CO2, has a global warming potential 28-34 times greater over a 100-year period. Agricultural practices, particularly livestock production and rice cultivation, along with fossil fuel extraction and waste management, are major methane sources. Deforestation and land-use changes compound the problem by reducing carbon sinks while simultaneously releasing stored carbon. The feedback mechanisms triggered by these primary drivers—such as ice-albedo feedback, permafrost thawing, and changes in ocean circulation—amplify warming effects and create cascading impacts across Earth's climate system.

## Key Findings

### F1: Fossil Fuel Combustion as Primary Driver

**Finding**: Burning of coal, oil, and natural gas for energy and transportation accounts for approximately 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions, releasing roughly 37 billion tonnes of CO2 annually as of 2023.

**Evidence**: The Global Carbon Project's annual carbon budget reports consistently show fossil fuel combustion as the largest single source of anthropogenic CO2 emissions. Energy sector emissions have grown from about 20 billion tonnes CO2 in 1990 to 37 billion tonnes by 2023. The correlation between fossil fuel use and atmospheric CO2 concentration is supported by isotopic analysis (carbon-13/carbon-12 ratios) that definitively links atmospheric CO2 increases to fossil sources rather than natural sources.

**Sources**:
- Global Carbon Project, "Global Carbon Budget 2023"
- IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I (2021)
- Friedlingstein et al., "Global Carbon Budget 2023," Earth System Science Data

**Confidence**: 0.95
**Importance**: high

### F2: Methane Emissions from Agriculture and Energy

**Finding**: Methane emissions contribute approximately 0.5°C of the observed 1.1°C warming, with agriculture (livestock and rice) accounting for 40% and energy sector (oil/gas extraction) accounting for 35% of anthropogenic methane sources.

**Evidence**: Atmospheric methane concentrations have increased from 722 parts per billion (ppb) in pre-industrial times to over 1900 ppb currently. Livestock enteric fermentation produces 90-100 million tonnes of methane annually. Recent studies using satellite data (TROPOMI) have identified major methane leakage from oil and gas infrastructure, with some facilities emitting thousands of tonnes annually.

**Sources**:
- Global Methane Assessment, UN Environment Programme (2021)
- Saunois et al., "The Global Methane Budget 2000-2017," Earth System Science Data
- NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory methane data

**Confidence**: 0.90
**Importance**: high

### F3: Deforestation and Land-Use Change

**Finding**: Deforestation and land-use changes contribute approximately 4-5 billion tonnes of CO2 annually (11-13% of total emissions) while simultaneously reducing the planet's carbon sequestration capacity by eliminating forest sinks.

**Evidence**: Tropical deforestation, particularly in the Amazon, Congo Basin, and Southeast Asia, has accelerated over recent decades. The Amazon alone has transitioned from a net carbon sink absorbing 0.5 billion tonnes CO2 annually to approaching carbon neutrality or becoming a net source in some regions. Satellite monitoring (Global Forest Watch) shows approximately 10 million hectares of tropical forest lost annually between 2015-2020.

**Sources**:
- Hansen et al., "High-Resolution Global Maps of 21st-Century Forest Cover Change," Science
- Gatti et al., "Amazonia as a carbon source linked to deforestation and climate change," Nature (2021)
- FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment

**Confidence**: 0.88
**Importance**: high

### F4: Industrial Processes and Cement Production

**Finding**: Industrial processes, particularly cement and steel production, contribute 5-6 billion tonnes of CO2 annually through both energy use and chemical reactions that release CO2 as a byproduct.

**Evidence**: Cement production alone accounts for 8% of global CO2 emissions through the calcination process (heating limestone releases CO2) and energy requirements. The chemical reaction CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 is inherent to cement production. Global cement production has tripled since 1990, driven primarily by infrastructure development in China and other developing nations.

**Sources**:
- International Energy Agency, "Cement Technology Roadmap"
- Andrew, "Global CO2 emissions from cement production," Earth System Science Data
- Lehne & Preston, "Making Concrete Change: Innovation in Low-carbon Cement and Concrete," Chatham House

**Confidence**: 0.92
**Importance**: medium

### F5: Transportation Sector Emissions

**Finding**: Transportation accounts for approximately 8 billion tonnes of CO2 annually (20% of global emissions), with road transport representing 75% of transport emissions and aviation contributing 12%.

**Evidence**: Global vehicle fleet has exceeded 1.4 billion vehicles, with internal combustion engines dominating. Aviation emissions have grown at 2.5% annually pre-pandemic, with additional climate impact from non-CO2 effects (contrails, NOx) that may double or triple the warming effect. Shipping contributes nearly 1 billion tonnes CO2 annually using heavy fuel oil.

**Sources**:
- IEA Global Energy Review, Transport Sector Analysis
- Lee et al., "The contribution of global aviation to anthropogenic climate forcing," Atmospheric Environment
- International Maritime Organization, Fourth IMO GHG Study

**Confidence**: 0.91
**Importance**: high

### F6: Nitrous Oxide from Agricultural Practices

**Finding**: Agricultural use of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers and manure management releases approximately 7 million tonnes of nitrous oxide annually, contributing significantly to warming given N2O's global warming potential of 265-298 times that of CO2.

**Evidence**: Global fertilizer use has increased from 110 million tonnes nitrogen in 1990 to over 190 million tonnes by 2020. Only 40-60% of applied nitrogen is taken up by crops, with the remainder lost through volatilization, leaching, and conversion to N2O. Atmospheric N2O has increased from 270 ppb pre-industrial to 335 ppb currently.

**Sources**:
- Tian et al., "A comprehensive quantification of global nitrous oxide sources and sinks," Nature (2020)
- Davidson, "The contribution of manure and fertilizer nitrogen to atmospheric nitrous oxide," Environmental Research Letters
- FAO fertilizer statistics

**Confidence**: 0.87
**Importance**: medium

### F7: Fluorinated Greenhouse Gases from Industrial Applications

**Finding**: Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) contribute approximately 1-2% of total greenhouse gas emissions but have extremely high global warming potentials (140 to 23,500 times CO2) and long atmospheric lifetimes.

**Evidence**: HFC emissions have grown rapidly as replacements for ozone-depleting substances, increasing from near zero in 1990 to over 400 million tonnes CO2-equivalent by 2020. SF6 used in electrical equipment has a global warming potential of 23,500 and atmospheric lifetime of 3,200 years. The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol aims to phase down HFC production.

**Sources**:
- Velders et al., "Future atmospheric abundances and climate forcings from scenarios of global HFC emissions," Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics
- UNEP Ozone Secretariat, Kigali Amendment reports
- NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index

**Confidence**: 0.89
**Importance**: medium

### F8: Feedback Mechanisms Amplifying Warming

**Finding**: Climate feedback mechanisms, including ice-albedo feedback, permafrost carbon release, and water vapor amplification, are accelerating warming beyond direct greenhouse gas forcing, with potential to add 0.2-0.4°C additional warming by 2100.

**Evidence**: Arctic sea ice has declined at 13% per decade since 1979, reducing surface reflectivity and increasing heat absorption. Permafrost regions contain 1,500 billion tonnes of carbon, with current thawing releasing 300-600 million tonnes CO2-equivalent annually. Water vapor, increasing at 7% per degree Celsius warming (Clausius-Clapeyron relation), acts as a powerful amplifying feedback.

**Sources**:
- IPCC AR6, Chapter 7: "The Earth's Energy Budget, Climate Feedbacks and Climate Sensitivity"
- Schuur et al., "Climate change and the permafrost carbon feedback," Nature
- Stroeve & Notz, "Changing state of Arctic sea ice," Nature Climate Change

**Confidence**: 0.85
**Importance**: high

## Key Insights

1. **Fossil fuel dominance**: The overwhelming majority (75%) of climate change is attributable to fossil fuel combustion, making energy system transformation the most critical intervention point for mitigation efforts.

2. **Multiple greenhouse gas sources**: While CO2 receives primary attention, methane and nitrous oxide from agriculture and industrial processes contribute substantially to warming and offer different mitigation opportunities with potentially faster climate benefits due to shorter atmospheric lifetimes.

3. **Interconnected systems**: Climate drivers are deeply embedded in fundamental economic activities (energy, food production, manufacturing, transportation), requiring systemic rather than isolated solutions.

4. **Accelerating feedbacks**: Natural feedback mechanisms are beginning to amplify human-caused warming, creating urgency for rapid emissions reductions before tipping points are crossed.

5. **Sectoral variation**: Different sectors contribute through different mechanisms (energy through combustion, agriculture through biological processes, industry through chemical reactions), requiring sector-specific mitigation strategies.

## Knowledge Gaps

- Precise quantification of methane emissions from natural gas infrastructure, with recent satellite observations suggesting significant underestimation in bottom-up inventories
- Magnitude and timing of carbon release from thawing permafrost under different warming scenarios
- Net climate impact of land-use changes in tropical regions where deforestation, reforestation, and agricultural intensification occur simultaneously
- Contribution of emerging sources such as cryptocurrency mining and AI data centers to global energy-related emissions
- Effectiveness of natural climate solutions (reforestation, soil carbon sequestration) at scale and their permanence under continued warming
