# Research: Main Causes of Climate Change

**Subtopic ID**: ST-E1-3
**Epoch**: 1
**Research Date**: 2026-04-23

## Overview

Climate change is primarily driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, with carbon dioxide (CO2) from fossil fuel combustion being the dominant contributor. Since the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric CO2 concentrations have increased from approximately 280 parts per million (ppm) to over 420 ppm as of 2023, representing a 50% increase. This unprecedented rise correlates directly with human activities including energy production, transportation, industrial processes, and land-use changes. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has established with very high confidence that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, with observed warming of approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels. Beyond CO2, other greenhouse gases including methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and fluorinated gases contribute significantly to radiative forcing. Methane, though shorter-lived than CO2, has a global warming potential 28-34 times greater over a 100-year period. Agricultural practices, particularly livestock production and rice cultivation, along with fossil fuel extraction and waste management, are major methane sources. Deforestation and land-use changes compound the problem by reducing carbon sinks while simultaneously releasing stored carbon. The feedback mechanisms triggered by these primary causes—such as ice-albedo feedback, permafrost thawing, and changes in ocean circulation—amplify warming effects and create cascading impacts across Earth's climate system.

## Key Findings

### F1: Fossil Fuel Combustion as Primary Driver

**Finding**: Burning of coal, oil, and natural gas for energy and transportation accounts for approximately 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions and is the single largest contributor to climate change.

**Evidence**: Global fossil fuel combustion releases approximately 36-37 billion tonnes of CO2 annually. The energy sector alone contributes about 73% of total greenhouse gas emissions, with electricity and heat production (25%), transportation (16%), and manufacturing (12%) being the largest subsectors. Atmospheric measurements show a clear isotopic signature (lower C-13/C-12 ratio) confirming fossil fuel origins of increased CO2.

**Sources**:
- IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), Working Group III, 2022
- Global Carbon Project, Annual Carbon Budget reports
- International Energy Agency (IEA), World Energy Outlook 2023

**Confidence**: 0.95
**Importance**: high

### F2: Deforestation and Land-Use Change

**Finding**: Tropical deforestation and land-use changes contribute 10-15% of global CO2 emissions while simultaneously reducing the planet's capacity to absorb carbon dioxide.

**Evidence**: Approximately 10 million hectares of forest are lost annually, primarily in tropical regions. Forests store roughly 861 gigatonnes of carbon globally, and deforestation releases 4-5 billion tonnes of CO2 per year. The Amazon rainforest, which historically acted as a carbon sink absorbing 2 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, has shown signs of becoming a net carbon source in some regions due to deforestation and degradation.

**Sources**:
- FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment 2020
- Nature Climate Change, Baccini et al., 2017
- Science, Hubau et al., 2020 - "Asynchronous carbon sink saturation in African and Amazonian tropical forests"

**Confidence**: 0.90
**Importance**: high

### F3: Agricultural Methane Emissions

**Finding**: Agriculture, particularly livestock production and rice cultivation, is responsible for approximately 40% of global methane emissions, a greenhouse gas with 28-34 times the warming potential of CO2 over 100 years.

**Evidence**: Enteric fermentation in ruminant livestock (cattle, sheep, goats) produces 90-100 million tonnes of methane annually. Rice paddies contribute an additional 30-40 million tonnes through anaerobic decomposition in flooded fields. Global livestock populations have increased dramatically—cattle populations exceed 1 billion head—driving methane emissions upward. Agricultural methane accounts for roughly 32% of total anthropogenic methane emissions.

**Sources**:
- EPA Global Non-CO2 Greenhouse Gas Emission Projections & Mitigation 2019-2050
- Nature, Saunois et al., 2020 - "The Global Methane Budget 2000-2017"
- IPCC AR6, Working Group I, Chapter 5

**Confidence**: 0.88
**Importance**: high

### F4: Industrial Processes and Cement Production

**Finding**: Industrial processes, particularly cement and steel production, contribute 5-10% of global CO2 emissions through both energy use and chemical reactions that release carbon.

**Evidence**: Cement production alone accounts for 8% of global CO2 emissions, releasing approximately 2.8 billion tonnes of CO2 annually. The calcination process (heating limestone to produce clinker) is inherently carbon-intensive, releasing CO2 as a byproduct of the chemical reaction itself, independent of energy sources. Steel production adds another 7-9% of global emissions. These process emissions are particularly challenging to eliminate as they're intrinsic to current production methods.

**Sources**:
- Nature Climate Change, Andrew, 2018 - "Global CO2 emissions from cement production"
- International Energy Agency, Technology Roadmap: Low-Carbon Transition in the Cement Industry
- World Steel Association, Steel's Contribution to a Low Carbon Future

**Confidence**: 0.92
**Importance**: medium

### F5: Methane Leakage from Fossil Fuel Infrastructure

**Finding**: Methane leakage from oil and gas extraction, processing, and distribution infrastructure contributes 70-90 million tonnes of methane annually, representing 20-25% of total anthropogenic methane emissions.

**Evidence**: Satellite observations and ground measurements reveal that methane leakage rates from fossil fuel infrastructure are 25-40% higher than previously estimated by industry reporting. Super-emitter sites, including abandoned wells, faulty equipment, and deliberate venting, account for a disproportionate share of emissions. The Permian Basin alone has been observed releasing methane at rates equivalent to the annual emissions of several European countries.

**Sources**:
- Science, Alvarez et al., 2018 - "Assessment of methane emissions from the U.S. oil and gas supply chain"
- Nature, Zhang et al., 2020 - "Quantifying methane emissions from the largest oil-producing basin in the United States"
- Environmental Research Letters, Lauvaux et al., 2022

**Confidence**: 0.85
**Importance**: high

### F6: Nitrous Oxide from Agricultural Fertilizers

**Finding**: Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer use in agriculture releases nitrous oxide (N2O), a greenhouse gas with 265 times the warming potential of CO2, contributing approximately 6% of total greenhouse gas forcing.

**Evidence**: Global fertilizer use has increased from 11 million tonnes of nitrogen in 1961 to over 110 million tonnes in 2020. Agricultural soils emit 4-6 million tonnes of N2O annually, with synthetic fertilizers responsible for roughly 60% of these emissions. Atmospheric N2O concentrations have increased 23% since pre-industrial times, from 270 ppb to 333 ppb, with agriculture as the dominant source.

**Sources**:
- Nature, Tian et al., 2020 - "A comprehensive quantification of global nitrous oxide sources and sinks"
- IPCC AR6, Working Group I, Chapter 5
- Environmental Research Letters, Davidson, 2009

**Confidence**: 0.87
**Importance**: medium

### F7: Fluorinated Gases from Industrial Applications

**Finding**: Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) used in refrigeration, air conditioning, and industrial processes have global warming potentials thousands of times greater than CO2 and are the fastest-growing category of greenhouse gases.

**Evidence**: Though representing only 2-3% of total greenhouse gas emissions by volume, fluorinated gases have grown at 4-5% annually. HFC-134a, commonly used in refrigeration, has a GWP of 1,430. SF6, used in electrical equipment, has a GWP of 23,500 and an atmospheric lifetime of 3,200 years. The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol aims to phase down HFC production by 80-85% by 2047.

**Sources**:
- NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index (AGGI)
- Nature Climate Change, Velders et al., 2015
- UNEP, Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol

**Confidence**: 0.90
**Importance**: medium

### F8: Black Carbon and Aerosol Emissions

**Finding**: Black carbon (soot) from incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, biofuels, and biomass burning contributes to warming through direct absorption of solar radiation and reduction of ice and snow albedo, with a climate forcing second only to CO2.

**Evidence**: Black carbon has a global warming potential approximately 460-1,500 times that of CO2 over a 100-year period. Annual emissions total 6-8 million tonnes, with residential solid fuel combustion (40%), diesel engines (20%), and open biomass burning (20%) as primary sources. When deposited on snow and ice, black carbon reduces surface albedo by 1-3%, accelerating melting. However, black carbon's short atmospheric lifetime (days to weeks) means emission reductions yield rapid climate benefits.

**Sources**:
- Journal of Geophysical Research, Bond et al., 2013 - "Bounding the role of black carbon in the climate system"
- Nature Geoscience, Ramanathan & Carmichael, 2008
- IPCC AR6, Working Group I, Chapter 6

**Confidence**: 0.82
**Importance**: medium

## Key Insights

1. **Fossil fuel dominance**: The overwhelming majority (75%) of climate change is attributable to fossil fuel combustion across energy, transportation, and industrial sectors, making energy system transformation the most critical intervention point for climate mitigation.

2. **Methane's outsized impact**: Despite shorter atmospheric lifetime, methane's high global warming potential and multiple emission sources (agriculture, fossil fuels, waste) make it a critical near-term target, with reduction potential to significantly slow warming rates within decades.

3. **Multiple reinforcing sources**: Climate change results from diverse, interconnected emission sources spanning energy, agriculture, industry, and land use, requiring comprehensive, multi-sector mitigation strategies rather than single-solution approaches.

4. **Process vs. energy emissions**: A significant portion of industrial emissions (cement, steel) comes from chemical processes themselves, not just energy use, presenting unique decarbonization challenges requiring technological innovation beyond renewable energy adoption.

5. **Non-CO2 gases matter**: While CO2 receives primary attention, methane, nitrous oxide, and fluorinated gases collectively contribute 25-30% of radiative forcing, with some offering faster climate benefits from emission reductions due to shorter atmospheric lifetimes.

## Knowledge Gaps

- Precise quantification of methane emissions from natural sources (wetlands, permafrost) versus anthropogenic sources remains uncertain, with satellite observations revealing discrepancies with bottom-up inventories
- The full extent of positive feedback mechanisms (permafrost carbon release, ocean carbon uptake reduction, forest carbon sink saturation) and their timing remains inadequately constrained
- Regional attribution of emissions, particularly in developing economies with limited monitoring infrastructure, creates uncertainty in global carbon budgets
- The climate impact of aerosols remains the largest source of uncertainty in radiative forcing calculations, with cooling effects potentially masking some warming
- Tipping point thresholds for irreversible changes in major Earth systems (ice sheets, ocean circulation, rainforests) lack precise determination
