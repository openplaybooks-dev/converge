# Research: Main Causes of Climate Change

**Subtopic ID**: ST-E1-2
**Epoch**: 1
**Research Date**: 2026-04-23

## Overview

Climate change is primarily driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, with carbon dioxide (CO2) from fossil fuel combustion being the dominant contributor. Since the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric CO2 concentrations have increased from approximately 280 parts per million (ppm) to over 420 ppm as of 2023, representing a 50% increase. This unprecedented rise correlates directly with human activities including energy production, transportation, industrial processes, and land-use changes. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has established with high confidence that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, with observed warming of approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels. Beyond CO2, other greenhouse gases including methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and fluorinated gases contribute significantly to radiative forcing. Methane, though shorter-lived than CO2, has a global warming potential 28-34 times greater over a 100-year period. Agricultural practices, particularly livestock production and rice cultivation, along with fossil fuel extraction and waste management, are major methane sources. Deforestation and land-use changes compound the problem by reducing carbon sinks while simultaneously releasing stored carbon. The feedback mechanisms triggered by these primary drivers—such as ice-albedo feedback, permafrost thawing, and changes in cloud formation—amplify warming effects, creating a complex system of interconnected causes that drive contemporary climate change.

## Key Findings

### F1: Fossil Fuel Combustion as Primary Driver

**Finding**: Burning of coal, oil, and natural gas for energy and transportation accounts for approximately 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions, making it the single largest contributor to anthropogenic climate change.

**Evidence**: Global fossil fuel combustion releases approximately 36-37 billion tonnes of CO2 annually. The energy sector alone contributes about 73% of total emissions, with electricity and heat production (31%), transportation (15%), and manufacturing (12%) being the largest subsectors. Atmospheric measurements show a clear isotopic signature (lower C-13/C-12 ratio) confirming fossil fuel origins of increased atmospheric CO2.

**Sources**:
- IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), Working Group III, 2022
- Global Carbon Project, Annual Carbon Budget reports
- International Energy Agency (IEA), World Energy Outlook 2023

**Confidence**: 0.95
**Importance**: high

### F2: Deforestation and Land-Use Change

**Finding**: Deforestation and land-use changes contribute 10-15% of global CO2 emissions while simultaneously reducing the planet's capacity to absorb carbon, creating a dual negative impact on atmospheric CO2 concentrations.

**Evidence**: Approximately 10 million hectares of forest are lost annually, primarily in tropical regions. Forests store an estimated 861 gigatonnes of carbon globally, and their destruction releases this stored carbon while eliminating crucial carbon sinks. The Amazon rainforest, which historically absorbed 2 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, has shown signs of becoming a net carbon source in some regions due to deforestation and degradation.

**Sources**:
- FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment 2020
- Nature Climate Change, Gatti et al., "Amazonia as a carbon source linked to deforestation and climate change" (2021)
- World Resources Institute, Global Forest Watch data

**Confidence**: 0.90
**Importance**: high

### F3: Agricultural Methane Emissions

**Finding**: Agriculture, particularly livestock production and rice cultivation, accounts for approximately 40% of global methane emissions, contributing significantly to short-term warming due to methane's high global warming potential.

**Evidence**: Enteric fermentation in ruminant livestock produces approximately 90-100 million tonnes of methane annually. Rice paddies contribute an additional 30-40 million tonnes through anaerobic decomposition in flooded fields. With global meat consumption projected to increase 70% by 2050, agricultural methane emissions pose an accelerating threat. Methane's atmospheric concentration has increased 150% since pre-industrial times, with agriculture being the largest anthropogenic source.

**Sources**:
- EPA Global Non-CO2 Greenhouse Gas Emission Projections & Mitigation 2019-2050
- Nature, Saunois et al., "The Global Methane Budget 2000-2017" (2020)
- FAO, "Tackling Climate Change Through Livestock" report

**Confidence**: 0.88
**Importance**: high

### F4: Industrial Processes and Cement Production

**Finding**: Industrial processes, particularly cement and steel production, contribute approximately 8-10% of global CO2 emissions through both energy consumption and chemical reactions that release CO2 as a byproduct.

**Evidence**: Cement production alone accounts for 8% of global CO2 emissions, releasing approximately 2.8 billion tonnes of CO2 annually. The calcination process (heating limestone to produce clinker) is inherently carbon-intensive, releasing one tonne of CO2 for every tonne of cement produced, independent of energy source. Steel production adds another 7-9% of global emissions. These process emissions are particularly challenging to eliminate as they are intrinsic to current production methods.

**Sources**:
- Nature, Lehne & Preston, "Making Concrete Change: Innovation in Low-carbon Cement and Concrete" (2018)
- IEA Technology Roadmap: Low-Carbon Transition in the Cement Industry
- Material Economics, "Industrial Transformation 2050" report

**Confidence**: 0.92
**Importance**: high

### F5: Permafrost Thawing and Carbon Feedback Loops

**Finding**: Arctic permafrost contains an estimated 1,500-1,700 gigatonnes of carbon, approximately twice the amount currently in the atmosphere, and warming-induced thawing is creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates climate change.

**Evidence**: Permafrost temperatures have increased by 0.3°C globally since 2007, with some regions experiencing increases of 1°C or more. As permafrost thaws, microbial decomposition of previously frozen organic matter releases both CO2 and methane. Current models project that permafrost thawing could release 50-250 gigatonnes of carbon by 2100, potentially adding 0.2-0.4°C to global warming. This represents a self-reinforcing feedback mechanism where warming causes emissions that cause further warming.

**Sources**:
- Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, Schuur et al., "Permafrost and Climate Change" (2022)
- NOAA Arctic Report Card, annual assessments
- Nature, Turetsky et al., "Permafrost collapse is accelerating carbon release" (2019)

**Confidence**: 0.85
**Importance**: high

### F6: Transportation Sector Emissions

**Finding**: The transportation sector contributes approximately 15-20% of global CO2 emissions, with road transport accounting for 75% of transport emissions, and aviation and shipping presenting particularly challenging decarbonization problems.

**Evidence**: Global transportation emits approximately 8 billion tonnes of CO2 annually. Road vehicles account for 6 billion tonnes, with passenger cars responsible for 45% and freight trucks 30%. Aviation contributes 2-3% of global emissions but has a disproportionate climate impact due to non-CO2 effects (contrails, NOx emissions at altitude). International shipping adds another 2-3%. Vehicle numbers are projected to double by 2050, particularly in developing economies, threatening to offset efficiency gains.

**Sources**:
- IEA Global Energy Review: CO2 Emissions in 2021
- ICCT (International Council on Clean Transportation) reports
- Nature Climate Change, Lee et al., "The contribution of global aviation to anthropogenic climate forcing" (2021)

**Confidence**: 0.91
**Importance**: high

### F7: Nitrous Oxide from Agricultural Fertilizers

**Finding**: Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer use releases nitrous oxide (N2O), a greenhouse gas with 265 times the warming potential of CO2 over 100 years, contributing approximately 6% of total anthropogenic greenhouse gas forcing.

**Evidence**: Agricultural soils are the largest source of N2O emissions, releasing approximately 4-5 million tonnes of N2O annually (equivalent to 1.2-1.5 billion tonnes CO2-equivalent). Fertilizer use has increased 800% since 1960, with current application rates of 110 million tonnes of nitrogen annually. Only 40-60% of applied nitrogen is taken up by crops, with the remainder lost through volatilization, leaching, and conversion to N2O. Atmospheric N2O concentrations have increased 20% since pre-industrial times.

**Sources**:
- Nature, Tian et al., "A comprehensive quantification of global nitrous oxide sources and sinks" (2020)
- IPCC Special Report on Climate Change and Land (2019)
- Environmental Research Letters, Davidson, "Representative concentration pathways and mitigation scenarios for nitrous oxide" (2012)

**Confidence**: 0.87
**Importance**: medium

### F8: Coal-Fired Power Generation

**Finding**: Coal combustion for electricity generation is the single largest source of CO2 emissions globally, accounting for approximately 30% of energy-related CO2 emissions despite generating only 36% of global electricity.

**Evidence**: Coal power plants emit approximately 10 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, nearly double the emissions from natural gas generation per unit of electricity produced. Coal's carbon intensity (approximately 1,000 grams CO2 per kWh) is 2-3 times higher than natural gas and orders of magnitude higher than renewable sources. Despite declining use in developed nations, coal capacity continues to expand in Asia, with China, India, and Southeast Asian nations adding new coal plants. Over 2,000 coal plants remain operational globally.

**Sources**:
- IEA Coal 2023 Report
- Global Energy Monitor, Global Coal Plant Tracker
- Science, Tong et al., "Committed emissions from existing energy infrastructure jeopardize 1.5°C climate target" (2019)

**Confidence**: 0.93
**Importance**: high

## Key Insights

1. **Fossil fuel dominance**: The overwhelming majority (75%) of climate change is attributable to fossil fuel combustion across energy, transportation, and industrial sectors, making energy system transformation the most critical intervention point for climate mitigation.

2. **Multiple greenhouse gas challenge**: While CO2 receives primary attention, methane and nitrous oxide from agricultural and industrial sources contribute significantly to warming, with methane's high potency making it a critical target for near-term climate action despite its shorter atmospheric lifetime.

3. **Self-reinforcing feedback mechanisms**: Natural systems like permafrost thawing and forest degradation are transitioning from carbon sinks to sources, creating positive feedback loops that amplify anthropogenic emissions and reduce the remaining carbon budget for limiting warming to 1.5-2°C.

4. **Sectoral interdependencies**: Climate change drivers are deeply interconnected—energy systems power industrial processes, agriculture depends on fossil-fuel-derived fertilizers, and land-use changes are driven by agricultural expansion—requiring integrated, systems-level solutions rather than isolated interventions.

5. **Lock-in effects from infrastructure**: Existing fossil fuel infrastructure (power plants, vehicles, industrial facilities) represents committed future emissions spanning decades, with current infrastructure alone potentially exhausting the 1.5°C carbon budget, highlighting the urgency of halting new fossil fuel investments.

## Knowledge Gaps

- Precise quantification of feedback loop magnitudes and tipping point thresholds, particularly for permafrost carbon release, ice sheet dynamics, and Amazon rainforest dieback
- Regional attribution of emissions sources and their relative contributions to observed warming patterns, especially for aerosol effects and land-use changes in tropical regions
- Effectiveness and scalability of emerging mitigation technologies (direct air capture, green hydrogen, sustainable aviation fuels) at the gigatonne scale required for meaningful climate impact
- Socioeconomic pathways and policy mechanisms that can achieve rapid decarbonization while ensuring just transitions for affected communities and industries
- Interaction effects between multiple simultaneous interventions and potential unintended consequences of large-scale climate solutions
